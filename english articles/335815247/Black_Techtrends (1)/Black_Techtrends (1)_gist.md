This article explores the often-overlooked challenges of adopting and implementing learning management systems, or LMSs, in higher education. While most institutions focus heavily on selecting the “right” LMS, the authors argue that the differences between available systems are minimal—so much so that the choice of platform is far less important than how well it is adopted and used. Most LMSs offer similar core features: quizzes, discussion forums, grade books, scheduling tools, and collaborative spaces. In fact, research suggests that many LMSs differ primarily in marketing rather than functionality. Given this standardization, the real challenge lies not in selection, but in successful implementation—the “other side of the LMS.”

The central problem the paper addresses is why, despite widespread availability and institutional investment, many LMSs fail to achieve full adoption by faculty and staff. The authors propose that the key issue isn’t the technology itself, but the human and organizational factors that influence how it’s received. Their guiding research question is: How can institutions increase the likelihood of successful LMS adoption and implementation?

To answer this, the authors draw on Everett Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations theory, which identifies five key attributes that influence whether new technologies are adopted: compatibility, relative advantage, trialability, observability, and complexity. These concepts are applied directly to the LMS context. For example, compatibility means aligning the LMS with faculty values, teaching styles, and institutional culture. Since professors come from diverse disciplines with unique pedagogical needs—from biology labs to language instruction—the system must be presented as flexible enough to support varied teaching methods. Without strong institutional backing and clear integration into the academic mission, even the most robust LMS will struggle to gain traction.

Relative advantage refers to whether the LMS offers clear benefits over existing methods. If faculty perceive it as time-consuming, unrewarded, or administratively burdensome, its advantages diminish. Issues like lack of compensation for online course development or concerns over intellectual property can undermine motivation to adopt. Cost is also a factor; licensing fees can range from $60,000 to millions, and without measurable returns, stakeholders may question the investment. Open-source alternatives like Moodle offer lower upfront costs, but they still require significant technical support and training.

Trialability—the ability to test the system on a small scale—emerges as a crucial strategy. Encouraging faculty to use the LMS in blended courses before moving to fully online instruction allows them to build confidence gradually. Similarly, observability plays a powerful role: when successes are visible and shared across campus, adoption spreads more easily. Showcasing effective uses of the LMS, celebrating early adopters, and minimizing publicized failures help build momentum. Conversely, technical problems—like lost student exam data—can quickly erode trust and stall progress.

The authors emphasize that complexity is a major barrier. Implementing an LMS involves coordination across academic, administrative, and technical units, and poor planning or insufficient support can doom the effort. Technical inefficiencies, such as difficult integrations or lack of plug-and-play features, add to the burden. Change agents—those leading the rollout—must shield users from unnecessary complexity while ensuring adequate training and ongoing support.

In conclusion, the authors stress that successful LMS adoption depends less on the software chosen and more on strategic, human-centered implementation. They recommend that change agents read widely about LMSs, visit other institutions that have succeeded, assess how the system fits their long-term vision, and adopt with a clear timeline. The significance of this work lies in shifting the conversation from technical comparison to organizational readiness. By focusing on adoption factors rather than product features, institutions can avoid common pitfalls and fully realize the potential of digital learning environments. Future efforts should continue refining support structures and customizability to make LMSs more adaptable to diverse educational needs. Overall, this paper makes a strong contribution by reframing LMS implementation as a socio-technical challenge—one that requires leadership, communication, and sustained support to overcome.
