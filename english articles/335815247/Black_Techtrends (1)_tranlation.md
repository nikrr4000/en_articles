Learning management systems, or LMSs, are now a cornerstone of online and blended education in higher education. While they offer tools like quizzes, discussion forums, scheduling, and grading, the reality is that most LMS platforms are remarkably similar in their core functionality. Research shows that the differences between them often come down to minor features—like the ability to download forum posts or record live sessions—rather than transformative capabilities. In fact, some experts argue that the real distinctions between LMSs lie more in marketing than in meaningful technological innovation. This standardization means that choosing one LMS over another is no longer the critical decision it’s often made out to be. Instead, the key challenge lies not in selection, but in adoption and implementation—the “other side of the LMS.”

The central issue this article addresses is why, despite widespread availability and institutional investment, many LMSs fail to achieve full integration into teaching practices. The authors argue that the problem isn’t the software itself, but how it’s introduced and supported within academic environments. Drawing on Everett Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations theory, they identify five key factors that influence whether an innovation like an LMS will be successfully adopted: compatibility, relative advantage, trialability, observability, and complexity.

Compatibility is crucial—faculty are more likely to adopt an LMS if it aligns with their teaching styles, content areas, and institutional culture. Since professors come from diverse disciplines with different pedagogical needs, a one-size-fits-all system can feel restrictive. For example, biology instructors may need virtual labs, while language teachers benefit from audio tools. Change agents—those leading the adoption process—must demonstrate how the LMS can be adapted or integrated with external tools to meet these varied needs. Institutional support also plays a major role; without clear backing, resources, and a long-term strategic vision, adoption efforts often stall.

Relative advantage refers to whether the LMS offers clear benefits over previous methods. If faculty perceive the system as saving time, improving instruction, or simplifying administration, they’re more likely to use it. However, many report the opposite: excessive preparation time, lack of compensation, and concerns over intellectual property reduce the perceived value. Cost is another factor—LMS licenses can run into millions of dollars, and without measurable returns, the investment may not be justified. Open-source alternatives like Moodle offer lower upfront costs, but they still require significant technical support and staffing.

Trialability allows users to test the system on a small scale before full rollout. Encouraging faculty to use the LMS in blended courses—partly online, partly face-to-face—can reduce anxiety and build confidence. Phased implementation in departments or programs where success is likely also increases the chances of broader adoption later.

Observability is about visibility—when successes are shared, others are more likely to follow. Change agents should create opportunities for faculty to showcase their work, celebrate wins, and share positive experiences. Conversely, highly visible failures—like lost student exam data—can severely damage trust and slow adoption. Strong technical support is essential here, not just for functionality, but for maintaining user satisfaction and confidence.

Complexity remains a major barrier. LMS implementation is inherently complex, and many institutions underestimate the resources needed. Vendors often fail to provide user-friendly, plug-and-play systems, leaving institutions to manage integration on their own. Change agents must simplify the process as much as possible and shield users from unnecessary technical hurdles.

In conclusion, the authors emphasize that successful LMS adoption depends less on which system is chosen and more on how it’s introduced and supported. They recommend that change agents read widely about LMSs, visit other institutions that have implemented them successfully, assess their own institutional needs, and adopt with a clear timeline. Ultimately, the goal is to shift focus from the technology itself to the human and organizational factors that determine whether it will be used effectively. By addressing the “other side of the LMS,” institutions can move beyond mere implementation to meaningful integration in teaching and learning.
